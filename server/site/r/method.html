The method · How Kleos measures

Seconds, not sentences.

People decide how you come across in the first moments, mostly from how you sound and how you hold yourself. That part can be measured. Measured, it can be trained.

I · Why it matters

The first half-minute is not small talk.

In 1993, Nalini Ambady and Robert Rosenthal showed strangers silent clips of teachers, some only a few seconds long. Their snap ratings lined up with the ones the teachers’ own students gave after a full term.1 Psychologists now call these thin slices, and the effect has held up across classrooms, clinics and boardrooms.

It isn’t only about being liked. When the same lab played ten-second clips of surgeons talking to patients, with the words filtered out so only the tone was left, the surgeons whose voices sounded more dominant were more likely to have been sued for malpractice.2 In job interviews, the impression formed in the first minutes of rapport shapes how interviewers score the formal answers that follow.3 And when MIT researchers measured 138 recorded mock interviews, features a computer could count, such as fluency, filler words and smiling, predicted how people were rated.4

None of that is about looks. It’s pace, pauses, where your voice goes at the end of a sentence, whether your eyes stay with the person. Those are habits, and habits move with practice.

II · What is measured

Numbers from the recording, not an opinion.

Every word gets a timestamp. Pitch and loudness are tracked through each sentence. On camera, face and posture landmarks show where your eyes go. Each result is placed against a range. Ranges marked research follow published findings; the rest are Kleos working ranges, labelled as such until a validation study backs them.

III · Judged, and never

Where judgement comes in, it says so.

Judged

One part is opinion: what you said. Did you answer the question, say something specific and your own, and finish the thought? Poise writes that read and the three tells, must point at something measured or quoted for each one, and it’s labelled judged wherever it appears.

Never

Kleos doesn’t rate your face, body, height, accent or the natural sound of your voice; only what you can train. It isn’t a medical or psychological test and diagnoses nothing. The video is measured and deleted, and the numbers and the read stay with you, unless you choose to help train Poise; then takes are kept for up to a year for rating, and removed when you say so.

IV · A number we don’t use

93%

“Ninety-three per cent of communication is nonverbal.”

You’ve heard it. It comes from two small 1967 experiments in which people heard single words said in different tones, and its author has said it was never meant for ordinary conversation. Words matter. That’s why Kleos reads what you said as well as how you said it, and keeps the two apart.

V · The limits

One take is a snapshot.

Light, a cheap microphone or a strange question all move the numbers. That’s why the Mirror asks the same question every week, so your takes compare like with like, and why the Index carries a range rather than pretending to a single decimal.

The next step is a rater study: real strangers rating thirty-second takes, so every Kleos range can be checked against what people actually perceive. Until then, those ranges say they’re ours.
